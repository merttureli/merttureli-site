Field study 01 / CHARGER / Union College Rocket Team / 2026

I founded the Union College Rocket Team and led it as captain and chief engineer. CHARGER, the team's 2026 rocket, dropped a sensor payload at apogee, streamed five channels of telemetry to a handheld ground station, and won the Deployable Sensor Payload event at Battle of the Rockets 2026 PAGE. This page flies it again from the CAD, in slow motion, with and without its four fins.

A rigid-body flight model computes the pitch motion with Barrowman's stability method, and a lattice Boltzmann section through the pitch plane shows the air meeting the fins at an angle of attack. A gust or a crosswind tips the nose; the fins, aft of the center of gravity, push it back.

02 / Appendix

How it was computed

  1. A 2D slice, not 3D

    In the slice the air can only pass over and under the body, not around it, so the slice overstates the body's share of the lift.

  2. Slow motion, not a film of the flow

    The lattice shows a steady picture of the air at the current angle of attack. Its timing does not follow the replay.

  3. A Reynolds number over 100 times too low

    The lattice runs at 900 to 1,500 SIM; the real rocket flies near 3 × 10⁵ at 70 m/s EST. So the lattice shows the air; it does not check the flight model, which flies the rocket.

Appendix A / Method

Method

Two models, two jobs. The flight model flies the rocket. The lattice shows the air; its forces belong to a 2D section and are not comparable with the rocket's (Method 03). Every number carries its source.

  1. 01Flight model

    A planar rigid body with three degrees of freedom (downrange, altitude, pitch), integrated with fourth-order Runge-Kutta at a fixed 1 ms step SIM. Normal-force slopes and centers of pressure for the ogive nose and the four trapezoidal fins follow Barrowman (1967), with the fin-body interference factor 1 + R / (s + R) STD. At large angles of attack the normal force uses sin α and adds body lift, 1.1 (Aplan / Aref) sin² α at the planform centroid, as OpenRocket does STD.

    Pitch damping sums CNα (x − xcg)² over the nose and the fins (Mandell, Caporaso and Bengen, Topics in Advanced Model Rocketry) and adds jet damping from the motor's mass flow with the variable-mass term (Thomson 1961) STD. The fin slope is incompressible; at the peak Mach number of 0.26 the compressible form gives 1.3 percent more SIM. Thrust is the AeroTech G77R curve from its published RASP file STD. Drag is one coefficient, 0.6339 FIT, found by bisection so that the still-air apogee equals the page's 1,033 ft PAGE. That apogee was simulated for the 37 in design, and there is no flight data, so the absolute apogee is not known; a component drag estimate for the CAD airframe gives 0.44 to 0.66 and 1,027 to 1,159 ft SIM. Apogee moves about 4.2 percent for 3 percent of motor impulse, 5.6 percent for 5 percent of mass and 3.2 percent for 10 percent of the drag coefficient SIM.

  2. 02Flow section

    A two-dimensional lattice Boltzmann model, D2Q9 with BGK collision (Chen and Doolen 1998) and a Smagorinsky subgrid model with C = 0.12 (Hou, Sterling, Chen and Doolen 1996) STD, runs on the graphics card in this tab. The rocket's section through the pitch plane is the solid, and its walls use halfway bounce-back. The flow is computed in the body's own frame: the walls stay at rest, the free stream at the edges turns with the body, and the body's pitch rate reaches the air as the Coriolis, centrifugal and Euler forces of a turning frame, added by Guo forcing (Guo, Zheng and Shi 2002) STD. The air arrives at the flight model's angle of attack.

    How the solver stays stable

    To stay stable with a relaxation time τ0 barely above 0.5 (0.504 at tier 3, set for a lattice Reynolds number of 1,500 at an inflow of 0.05 lattice units, the fastest the engine runs) EST the engine adds a regularized collision (Latt and Chopard 2006) and a positivity limiter (Brownlee, Gorban and Levesley 2007) STD, absorbing layers along the edges, and extra viscosity where the local speed passes 0.15 lattice units and for the first 2,000 steps after a reset EST. The outflow edges hold the density at 1, take the velocity of the cell inside and keep only the regularized part of its non-equilibrium (Latt, Chopard, Malaspinas, Deville and Michler 2008) STD. The section's forces count as developed once those steps are over, the inflow has reached the flight's angle and the air has traveled 1.5 body lengths since the last reset, about 10,100 steps at tier 1 at 0.05 lattice units SIM; until then the lab's lattice readouts show a dash. At the 0.05 cap the cells along the walls run at 10 to 25 percent more than the set viscosity, an effective Reynolds number of 1,200 to 1,360 SIM.

    Force and moment on the section come from momentum exchange at the wall links. They describe a 2D section, not the rocket (Method 03), so the panel shows them only on the lab view of this page (?lab), as means over one pass of the air along the body, labeled 2D SECTION ONLY / NOT COMPARABLE. The flight model flies the rocket. A lab toggle hands the pitching moment to the lattice instead; it shows what a 2D section would do and is not quantitative.

  3. 03What the section is not

    It is a 2D slice of a body of revolution. In 2D the air cannot pass around the body, only over and under it, so the section overstates the body's share of the lift, and the fins act as plates of unlimited span. The lattice viscosity is set for a Reynolds number of 1,500 at an inflow uin of 0.05 lattice units EST. Since Re = uin D / ν, the Reynolds number follows the inflow: 900 on the pad, rising to 1,500 at 0.05 lattice units, the fastest inflow the engine runs, which the flight reaches at 28 m/s SIM. The real rocket at 70 m/s is near 3 × 10⁵ (diameter 68 mm CAD, ν = 1.46 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s, the International Standard Atmosphere at sea level and 15 °C STD).

    What the section's numbers mean

    So the lattice's center of pressure and moment coefficient are 2D section values. In 2D the long body lifts like a wing along its whole length, with the center of pressure near its front quarter (thin-airfoil theory, Anderson, Fundamentals of Aerodynamics) STD. Measured on both page grids at 2 to 15°, the section's center of pressure sat 15 to 18 in from the nose, ahead of the CG, where Barrowman puts it at 29 to 33 in (32.94 in at small angles) SIM. A finer grid or a taller domain did not move it, and neither a slender-body nor a finite-wing correction closed the gap SIM. Taken as a 3D coefficient, the section's normal force was 16 to 35 times Barrowman's, and the page's domain, 0.47 rocket lengths tall, raised it about 1.7 times against a domain 3.2 times taller SIM. The section's pitch damping has the right sign, but at 1,380 to 2,010 against Barrowman's 445 it is not the rocket's; a pitching flat plate in the same body frame got 0.38 of its lift slope per unit of pitch rate, where theory gives 0.5 SIM. One cell inside the inflow edge the air arrives at 97.9 and 96.3 percent of the inflow speed at 5 and 10°, so the section's coefficients read about 4 and 8 percent low SIM. Single readings scatter by about 22 percent in Cn and 66 percent in Cm, so the lab rows show means over one pass of the air along the body SIM. The lab's lattice Cm is taken about the live CG, which moves forward as the propellant burns, as Barrowman's moment is. The lattice CP shows a dash below 1° of inflow angle, where the section's small offset from the rail buttons (Cn about −0.1 to −0.4 at 0°) would make it meaningless SIM. The lab toggle that lets the lattice moment fly the rocket therefore makes it diverge; it shows what a 2D section would do, not what CHARGER does.

    The page asks for an inflow in lattice units of uin = 0.03 + 0.05 V / (70 m/s), held between 0.03 and 0.10 EST. The engine runs the flow at 0.05 at most and, when the page asks for more, scales the body's pitch rate down in the same ratio, so the reduced pitch rate q D / V is kept EST. The 0.03 floor keeps the smoke moving on the pad, so the air there is not still. The inflow angle is held within ±35° EST: on the pad in a crosswind, and as the finless rocket tumbles, the section shows 35° while the flight model reports more, and the stage says so.

  4. 04Slow motion

    The replay changes speed with the flight. It runs at 1/8 of real time on the rail EST. From just before rail exit it slows to a rate chosen so that the wobble, from rail exit until the angle of attack stays under 2°, takes about 12 s on screen, held between 1/32 and 1/8 EST: 1/20 for the default launch SIM. At the first swing of the nose it stops for 1.5 s, marked HOLD, and it is back at real time by about T+1.6 s EST. For a rocket that turns over, the slow part runs until the pitch passes 60° EST. The replay pauses at apogee. The header shows the true rate at every moment, and the rate buttons in the panel override it. The flight model is stepped by screen time multiplied by the rate, so its events keep their real timing while frames take under 0.25 s; a longer frame advances it by 0.25 s times the rate. The lattice runs up to 24 steps per 60 Hz frame at tier 3 EST, fewer on faster displays and fewer when frames run long (the engine sheds steps to stay inside its 48 ms budget), whatever the playback rate, so the flow is a quasi-steady picture at the current angle of attack, not a time-accurate film of the flight.

  5. 05The rocket here and in the case study

    The case study and the CAD assembly agree on four trapezoidal fins, a body 2.68 in across, the fin root chord (2.76 in) and sweep (2.17 in) PAGE; the CAD measures the body at 2.677 in, the root chord at 2.76 in and the sweep at 2.17 in CAD. Shape and dimensions here come from the CAD, the mass from the page (2.2 lb) PAGE. Four differences remain.

    • Length. The CAD assembly is 46.49 in from nose tip to screw cap CAD; the case study gives 37 in PAGE. Which one is current is an open question; until it is settled, this page states both and takes the shape from the CAD.
    • CG. The case study gives 22 in from the nose for its 37 in design PAGE. Every part-wise estimate of the CAD airframe at the page mass puts it at 27.09 to 27.24 in EST, and this page flies 27.17 in EST. The shape here is the CAD's, and the 22 in CG belongs to the 37 in design, so the CG is the CAD's estimate too. Until CHARGER's CG is measured, this page flies the estimate; the panel's CG slider moves it.
    • Stability margin. At the CG estimate the margin is 2.16 cal SIM, 2.01 to 2.47 cal over the estimate's density scenarios SIM; at the case study's 22 in it would be 4.09 cal SIM. The case study gives 1.80 cal at 22 in for its 37 in airframe PAGE; that is the case study's figure, not a result of this study.
    • Fin height. The CAD fin span is 2.17 in CAD, against the case study's 2.36 in height PAGE.

  6. 06What the runs show

    With full fins the nose turns into the wind and the angle of attack settles within about a second SIM. With half fins the static margin is −2.41 cal at liftoff, which is unstable SIM; the CG moves forward as the propellant burns, but the margin is still −2.03 cal at burnout, and the rocket tumbles SIM.

    Without fins the margin is −8.78 cal SIM. Body lift acts at the planform centroid, 24.22 in from the nose SIM, ahead of the 27.17 in CG as well, so nothing turns the nose back: the rocket tumbles end over end, to an apogee near 220 ft SIM. At the case study's 22 in CG the centroid sat behind the CG, and the finless airframe settled at 20 to 30° and arced over instead SIM, so which of the two CHARGER would do depends on its CG (Method 05).

  7. 07Checks

    Both models were checked against published references, closed forms and an independent code (lab: Validation). The flight model's Barrowman terms matched the 1966 equations to 1e-6 SIM and Centuri's JAVELIN worked example to 0.12 percent SIM. RocketPy 1.13.0, a six-degree-of-freedom code flown with the same inputs at the case study's 22 in CG, landed within 0.006 percent of the apogee SIM and within 0.02° of the weathercock angle in a crosswind of 10 mph EST SIM. That checks the arithmetic, not the inputs: the CG this page flies is a part-wise estimate of the CAD airframe, 27.17 in EST, not a measurement, and the drag coefficient is fitted to a simulated apogee, not to flight data. The lattice reproduced channel flow at second order in the grid spacing SIM. With domain and grid effects removed, a cylinder at a Reynolds number of 100 STD gave a drag coefficient of 1.318 and a Strouhal number of 0.161 SIM, against 1.320 and 0.165 in published 2D results (Parvar et al. 2023, Table 1) STD. The gate, a cylinder 24 cells across placed 16 diameters behind the inflow edge of a 768 × 192 grid EST, read 1.369 and 0.164 once its shedding had saturated SIM; in the original 512 × 192 layout, 5.3 diameters behind the edge, it read 1.473 and 0.172, 11.6 and 4.2 percent high SIM. The side edges are open, so blockage corrections do not apply, and the staircase walls leave a grid error of several percent on a body 16 to 39 cells across SIM. The rocket's section did not agree with Barrowman: its center of pressure sat 15 to 18 in from the nose SIM, ahead of the CG, where Barrowman puts it at 29 to 33 in SIM, and no standard 3D correction closed the gap. So the section shows the air, and its forces are not a check on the flight model. The runs used software rendering (SwiftShader) and float32 CPU copies of the lattice step, so their speeds say nothing about a graphics card.

    Validation. The validation lab lists all 351 cases, each with its reference and source, the error, the tolerance set before the run and the result: 230 passed, 91 failed and 30 were informational. The 105 cases measured again after the fixes count at their new result; the rest are from before the fixes. Most of the fails are in the lattice tracks: the cylinder at the page's own grid and domain, and the rocket's 2D section against the 3D rocket.

    The flight model's tests

    The flight model passes its tests under node (node vx/js/flight/flight.test.mjs): the four-fin CNα and CP against a hand computation, the ogive nose CP at 0.466 of its length, the restoring sign of the moment, the still-air apogee within 1 percent of 1,033 ft after the fit, rail exit against its prediction, energy and NaN guards, determinism, and the lattice toggle (the lattice moment replaces Barrowman's through a 10 Hz low-pass, Barrowman's stays until a lattice moment arrives, and pitch damping is not added twice).

03 / Words used here

Words used here

Angle of attack (AOA, α)
The angle between where the nose points and where the air comes from. At 0° the rocket flies straight into the air.
Center of gravity (CG)
The balance point. This page puts CHARGER's 27.2 in from the nose at liftoff, an estimate from the CAD EST; the case study gives 22 in for its 37 in design PAGE. The point moves forward as the motor burns SIM.
Center of pressure (CP)
Where the air's sideways push on the whole rocket adds up. Fins far back pull it toward the tail.
Static margin, calibers
How far the CP sits behind the CG, counted in body diameters (1 cal = 2.68 in here CAD). A positive margin means the air turns the nose back; a negative one means it tips it further over.
Normal force
The sideways push of the air on the rocket.
Rail exit
The moment the forward rail button leaves the guide rail. Until the rocket clears the rail, the rail steers; after that, the fins do.
Weathercocking
A finned rocket turns into a crosswind, like a weathervane, and climbs upwind.
Barrowman's method
The standard hand calculation of a model rocket's CP and stability (Barrowman 1967).
Lattice Boltzmann section
Airflow computed on a grid of cells, each holding how much air moves in each of nine directions. Here it is a flat 2D slice through the rocket, shown for the picture, not used to fly it. Its forces are a 2D section's and do not check the flight model (Method 03).
Source tags
PAGE published on merttureli.com; CAD measured from the 3D model; EST chosen here; FIT tuned to match a published number; SIM worked out live in your browser; STD a textbook value.
