Project 03 of 07 . Test fixture design . SunThru

A fully printable three-point bend fixture

Some specimens are far too delicate for commercial bend fixtures, which are built for metals and plastics and will damage a fragile sample before the test even begins. SunThru needed a fixture matched to its own samples. I designed a fully printable 12-part jig in SolidWorks 2025, built entirely around gentle handling.

The load case

A three-point test is deliberately simple: carry the specimen on two supports a known distance apart, push down at mid-span, and record load against deflection. The whole value of the measurement rests on the specimen seeing that load and nothing else: no clamping, no twist, no stress concentration where the fixture happens to touch it. When the sample cannot take being clamped, the fixture itself becomes the thing that breaks it.

Constraints

  • Every part printable on the lab's FDM printer, with no machined components, so a revision costs an afternoon rather than a purchase order
  • 12 interlocking parts sharing one coordinate system, so dimension changes ripple predictably
  • Standard M3 hardware throughout, with horizontal cross-holes shaped as printable channels
  • Compliant contact geometry at the load nose and both supports, so the specimen sees a clean three-point load and nothing else

Twelve parts that stay in agreement

A 12-part fixture with interlocked dimensions is a maintenance problem before it is a design problem: change one dimension by hand and eleven other parts silently stop fitting, usually discovered after they are printed. I kept every part on a shared coordinate system and automated the rebuilds with SolidWorks VBA, so a dimension change regenerates the affected parts consistently, with each part's geometry checked against its expected volume before acceptance. That volume check is crude, and it catches the failure that actually happens: a feature that silently failed to rebuild.

Designing for the printer, not around it

The parts are shaped the way the printer builds them: geometry that accumulates in support-free layers, load paths running along the layers where it matters, and holes drawn as printable channels rather than as circles a drill would later fix. This is the part that generalises. Designing for the process beat designing around it.

In daily lab use. The open item is a published repeatability figure: a fixture that claims gentle handling should be able to prove it, and measuring that scatter is the next thing I would do with it.

What I learned

The robust design decisions were the ones that thought like the manufacturing process. The decision I am least sure about is printing the compliant contacts rather than using a moulded elastomer: printed compliance is tunable and free, but it is also a wear surface that changes slowly over months. Measuring that drift is the same job as measuring the repeatability.
