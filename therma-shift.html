Therma-Shift: a coaster that heats or cools, on one module

Existing options split into two bad camps: proprietary heated mugs that lock you into their drinkware, and unregulated USB warmers, some of them genuine fire hazards. The interesting question sits in between: one compact device that both heats and cools, works with whatever cup is on your desk, and can be trusted unattended.

The design

A thermoelectric module is a heat pump with no moving parts. Drive current one way and the top plate cools; reverse it and the plate heats. The TEC1-12703 sits between an aluminium top plate and a finned heatsink with a blower, an NTC thermistor at the plate closes the loop targeting a 2 degree F band, and a DRV8871 H-bridge lets the firmware command both power and polarity.

The real problem is the hot side

Making the cold face cold is easy. Keeping it cold is the engineering. A Peltier does not destroy heat, it moves it, and adds its own electrical losses on top; every watt pulled off the drink plus every watt burned doing so arrives at the hot face and has to leave. If it does not leave fast enough, efficiency collapses and the cold side warms back up. The cold side's performance is set almost entirely by how well the hot side is cooled, which is why most of the design is a heatsink and a blower rather than anything clever near the mug.

Validate the physics before the electronics

Before integrating any control loop I bench-tested the bare stack against a lab supply with K-type thermocouples on both faces. There is no point writing a controller for a plant that cannot reach the setpoint, and measuring first meant the control target was chosen from data rather than datasheet optimism.

  • At 12 V the cold face held 10.7 to 12 C with the heatsink at 53 to 56 C
  • Thermal equilibrium in about five minutes
  • A voltage sweep mapped cold-face temperature against input power to find the efficient operating window
  • Swapping the heatsink's stock adhesive pad for proper thermal paste produced a measurable gain: the interfaces matter as much as the module
  • Verdict: PASS, dual mode confirmed from the same stack by polarity

Safety, same instinct as the lab rig

The control loop regulates; separate firmware cutoffs watch for thermal runaway independently of it. The failure mode is specific: if the blower stalls or the firmware hangs while the module is driven hard, the hot side has nowhere to dump heat and the stack cooks itself. The cutoffs assume that day will come rather than trusting it will not.

The numbers above are open-loop bench results, labelled as such on purpose: what is proven so far is that the plant can reach the target. The staged firmware test suite is written and closed-loop integration is in progress.

What I learned

Thermal interfaces dominate the budget: the cheapest change in the whole project, paste instead of pad, produced one of the biggest measured gains. And this project is the other end of the same problem as my test rig: the rig exists to measure heat crossing a boundary, the coaster exists to move it, and the rig's lesson about not trusting inferred heat flow is demonstrated here, where the losses land exactly where they hurt.
