Project 02 of 07 . Test rig design . SunThru

A benchtop rig that ranks samples by thermal resistance

The company needed to compare flat samples thermally and had no way to do it. A supplier quoted $1,700 before shipping for comparable equipment. I proposed and designed a benchtop rig instead, end to end: enclosure, instrumentation, firmware, acceptance logic and the safety case. It cost $420 and runs unattended overnight.

The measurement

Thermal resistance is not something you read off a part. You hold a temperature difference across it and measure how much heat crosses. The rig is a reduced-scale hot box: about 55 degrees C of air on one face of the specimen, room air on the other, with the specimen sealed into an aperture between the two.

The decision that determines whether the numbers mean anything is how you measure the heat. The cheap approach infers it from heater power, which quietly assumes every watt went through the sample and not out through the walls, the seams, or the wiring. It did not.

A judgment call worth the money

My first instrumentation concept used a Peltier element as the heat-flux sensor, about $110 cheaper. I bench-tested it and it could not distinguish samples that were close together, which is precisely the case the rig exists to resolve. So I killed it and paid for a calibrated heat-flux sensor that reads actual watts per square metre through the specimen. On a $420 build, spending a quarter of the budget on one sensor needed justifying, and the justification is simple: a cheap rig that cannot tell two samples apart is not cheap, it is worthless.

Electronics and acceptance logic

  • ESP32 runs the heater control loop, the logging, and a live status page
  • Precision 24-bit ADC for the heat-flux signal, which is microvolts and needs real resolution
  • Four digital temperature sensors at 0.1 degree resolution: two in air, two on the specimen surfaces, so surface temperature is measured rather than assumed
  • Steady-state acceptance: a run only reports once the result has held within 1% for a full hour, so a number is never taken while the box is still drifting
  • CSV output plus a live page, so a run can be checked without stopping it

The acceptance rule is the part I would defend hardest. Anyone can read a sensor. The rig's value is that it refuses to report a number it has not earned, and it makes that call the same way every time, which is the entire point of a test rig.

Safety, because nobody is watching it

An overnight heater in a foam box is a fire risk, and the design assumes the firmware will fail. The protection is layered so that no single failure, including total loss of software, can let it overheat:

  • A physical power ceiling: the heater cannot exceed 37 W regardless of what it is told
  • A one-shot thermal fuse in series with a mechanical thermostat, both upstream of the controller, neither needing software to work
  • Firmware cutoffs on over-temperature and on sensor faults, including a reading that stops changing
  • A fused supply

Build and repeatability

  • Enclosure of 1 inch XPS foam: cheap, replaceable, thermally boring
  • Every precision part 3D printed in PETG, which survives the warm side better than PLA
  • Interchangeable printed cassettes set the test aperture, so a new specimen size does not mean a new rig
  • A printed jig repositions the heat-flux sensor to within 1 mm every run, because sensor placement is otherwise the largest source of run-to-run scatter
  • The electronics and firmware carry unchanged to a larger chamber; only the foam box and the printed frames change

The design went into service without changes to the parts list. My supervisor's written review: "I did not alter your design or parts because I believe your rig is already in a good place."

What I learned

Two things carried into everything I have built since. The sensor is the experiment: more design effort went into how the heat-flux sensor mounts and repositions than into the box around it, and that is the right ratio. And a rig should know when it is lying: steady-state detection, sensor-fault validation and the physical power ceiling are all the same instinct, which is to assume misbehaviour and make it visible or impossible.
