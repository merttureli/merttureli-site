Business cards · reference pass

The good ones are not about layout.

I designed nine cards across two attempts and you did not like any of them, which was the right call. Here is what I found when I stopped designing and went looking. Almost none of the memorable cards are memorable because of where the type sits. They are memorable because the card physically becomes the thing the person does.

What the best studios actually did 16 cards, Creative Boom

Named examples. The mechanic, not the praise.

Hey Studio Estampaciones Fuerte

A metal stamping company. The card carries three metallic strips that hint at the letters E and F. The client stamps metal, so the card is a stamped piece of metal. This is the one closest to you.

Alex Hunting Studio Wool

Fabric embossing, a deep clear-foil deboss and silver foil. You feel textile before you read a word. The card is the product.

DutchScot Sucre

The logo is imprinted on one side so it embosses through to the other. One press, two different cards depending which way up it lands. Nearly free, because it is one action.

Burak Kaynak Matière Noire

Letterpress on 100% cotton Lettra, then each card hand-wrapped with black cotton thread. The labour is the message.

Bond Pino

Not one card. The same mark on five colours, magenta through blue. A system that people want the set of.

Pentagram Momofuku

Brown paper and off-white stock, wordmarks that stretch, squash and jitter. The type misbehaves on purpose.

Every one of those is a physical idea, not an arrangement. Which is exactly what my nine cards were missing: they were all arrangements.

Six directions for you ranked, honestly

Each one follows the same rule: the card becomes the thing you do.

1. Cut the card to the fin

Die cut · your own DXF

Not a card with a fin printed on it. A card cut on the exact outline you sent to the laser cutter for the 2026 flight hardware, root tab and all. Estampaciones Fuerte stamps metal and their card is stamped metal. You cut parts, so your card is a part.

Nobody at a career fair has ever been handed a rocket fin. It does not fit a cardholder, which is the point: it goes on a desk.

Die fee once, then cheap. Runs from 50.

2. Blind deboss, no ink

Letterpress impression · zero ink

The fin, or your name, pressed into thick uncoated cotton with no ink at all. You feel it before you see it. This is the single most expensive looking thing you can do to a card and it uses no colour, which suits a site that is monochrome by decision.

The Sucre trick applies here for free: press it once and it embosses through to the reverse, so both faces carry it.

Needs 400gsm+ cotton. Minimum runs around 250.

3. One card per project

Collectable series · Bond's Pino move

Four cards, identical back, a different project on each front: CHARGER, the Robo-Catcher, the bend fixture, the pedals. Hand out whichever fits the conversation. Give a recruiter the rocket one if they mentioned aerospace.

People keep sets. They throw away singles. This is the cheapest idea on the list because it is just four print files.

No extra process. Four files, one order.

4. Score and fold, like a flat pattern

Folded card · sheet metal logic

A folded card is four faces instead of two, and for you it can be drawn as a flat pattern with a bend line, the way a sheet metal part is drawn before it is formed. Opens to give a project a proper caption instead of a phone number.

Honest risk: folded cards are twice the material and can read as a leaflet rather than a card. It only works if the fold is the idea.

Roughly double the stock. Common, no die fee.
SPOT UV

5. Black card, one thing catching light

Spot UV or foil on black · max 25% coverage

Matte black stock with the fin outline in clear gloss varnish. You cannot see it straight on. Tilt the card and the part appears. Invisible until it moves, which is a nice thing for a rocket to do.

Works on your existing ink palette because clear varnish adds no colour.

Spot UV is cheap. Foil costs a plate.
0.5 mm ALUMINIUM

6. Not paper at all

Thin metal or FR4 · the risky one

A thin aluminium or PCB-substrate card. It is the most literal version of the idea and it is the one I would push back on. The research is consistent that novelty materials read as "I bought a gimmick" unless the material is genuinely your material. You work in aerogel and printed polymer, not sheet metal.

Listed because you should see the whole range, not because I would print it.

Expensive per unit. High regret risk.

What I would actually print if it were mine

Direction 1 and 3, together. A die cut fin as the object you hand to somebody you want to remember you, and a normal rectangular set of four project cards for volume. The fin is the one that gets kept on a desk. The set is the one you can hand out fifty of in a day without flinching.

Direction 2, the blind deboss, is the most beautiful and the least necessary. Keep it for when someone else is paying.
